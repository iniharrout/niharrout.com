How to Set a Software Development Budget Before You Ask for Quotes

Most founders ask "how much will this cost?" before they have decided how much they are willing to spend. Flip the order. A budget you set first, split into phases and tied to outcomes, makes every quote easier to read and every scope decision easier to make. Here is the method I walk clients through.

On almost every first call, someone asks me what their app will cost. I understand why. But the more useful question comes first: what are you willing to spend to reach a specific outcome, and over what period? Once that is clear, the rest of the conversation is about choices, not guesses.

This guide is the method I use with founders and business owners to set a software budget before they request a single quote. I will not give price ranges here because they depend on your scope. For a starting range, use our project cost guide or the app cost calculator, then come back and shape that number into a plan.

The principle

A budget is not one number. It is at least four: what you spend to learn, what you spend to build the first release, what you spend to run it, and what you hold back for change. A quote usually covers only one of them.

Start with the outcome, not the feature list

Write down, in one or two sentences, what the software must change for your business. "Cut order entry time for our sales team" or "let customers book and pay without calling us" are outcomes. "An app with chat, a dashboard and AI" is a feature list, and feature lists grow until they fill whatever budget you have.

Then ask what that outcome is worth. If the software saves staff hours, estimate how many. If it brings revenue, estimate how much a realistic first year could bring. You do not need precision. You need a ceiling: the amount above which the project no longer makes sense. That ceiling is your total budget, and everything below is about spending it in the right order.

Split the budget into phases

Spending everything on a single big build is the most common budgeting mistake I see. Splitting the money across phases gives you decision points where you can stop, change course or invest more. The UK government uses a similar staged approach for its digital services, described in the GOV.UK Service Manual, and it works just as well for a startup or a mid-sized business.

PhaseWhat the money buysWhat you should have at the end
DiscoveryWorkshops, user interviews, a written scope, rough designsA PRD, a clickable prototype and a firmer estimate
First releaseDesign, engineering, testing and launch of the core workflowWorking software in front of real users
IterationFixes and the features users actually ask forA product shaped by usage, not assumptions
RunningHosting, third-party services, monitoring, updatesSoftware that stays healthy month after month
ReserveChanges nobody could see comingRoom to react without stopping the project

Discovery is the cheapest phase and the one that protects the rest. Our guide on the discovery phase in software development explains what it should produce. If a team skips it and jumps straight to a fixed price for a large build, the risk has not gone away. It is just hidden in the quote.

Size the first release to fit, not the other way round

Once you know how much the first release can take, cut the scope until it fits that number with room to spare. A useful test: if a feature were removed, could users still complete the main job? If yes, it waits. The first release should prove that the core workflow works and that people use it. Everything else is a later decision made with real data.

At Creuto a focused first working version is usually ready in about 4 weeks, which keeps the first phase small enough to judge quickly. You see real software early, and you decide what to fund next based on how it performs.

Budget for running costs from day one

The build price is a one-time number. Running costs arrive every month and many founders discover them only after launch. Put them in the budget as their own line:

  • Hosting and databases: grows with users and data.
  • Third-party services: maps, SMS, email, payment gateways, video, AI model usage. Each has its own pricing model.
  • App store accounts: Apple charges a yearly fee for the Apple Developer Program, and Google charges a one-time fee to register a Google Play developer account.
  • Monitoring and backups: error tracking, uptime checks, logs.
  • Maintenance: operating system updates, security patches, library upgrades and small fixes.

Ask every team you speak to for an estimate of monthly running costs at your expected usage, with the assumptions written down. Our guide to software maintenance after launch covers what that ongoing work includes.

Keep a reserve, and set rules for it

Every project learns something mid-way. A user test shows a screen is confusing. A partner's API behaves differently from its documentation. A regulation changes. A reserve held outside the quoted price lets you respond without renegotiating the whole project.

The key is rules. Agree in advance what the reserve can be spent on (changes from user feedback, integration surprises) and what it cannot (features someone simply thought of later). Unused reserve rolls into the iteration phase, which is where it does the most good anyway.

Tie payments to milestones you can see

How you pay shapes how a project runs. Large upfront payments for work you cannot inspect put all the risk on you. Payments tied to working milestones, such as a signed-off design, a demo on a test server, or a release in the app stores, keep both sides focused on visible progress.

The pricing model matters too. A fixed price suits a small, well-defined scope. Time and materials suits work that will change as you learn. Our comparison of fixed price and time and materials explains when each one fits. Whatever the model, make sure the code sits in your own accounts from day one. At Creuto it does, and ownership of custom code passes to you as each milestone is paid.

A budget checklist before you ask for quotes

  1. One or two sentences describing the outcome the software must deliver.
  2. A ceiling: the total you are willing to spend to reach that outcome.
  3. A split across discovery, first release, iteration, running costs and reserve.
  4. The core workflow the first release must support, and a list of what can wait.
  5. Expected users in the first year, so running costs can be estimated.
  6. Integrations you already know you need, such as payments, CRM or ERP.
  7. Any rules on data, regions or compliance.
  8. The payment structure you are comfortable with.

Share the budget range with the teams you speak to. A good partner will tell you what fits, what does not and where they would spend the money first. Our guide on what decides app development cost helps you read the answers and compare quotes fairly.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I budget for building an app?

It depends on scope, platforms, integrations and who builds it, so any single number without a scope is a guess. Start from the outcome you need and the amount you can afford to spend to reach it, then use our project cost guide and app cost calculator to check whether a first release fits.

How much contingency should a software budget have?

Keep a reserve that is separate from the quoted build price and agree in advance what it can be spent on, such as changes that come out of user testing. Projects with many integrations or unclear requirements need a larger reserve than a small, well-scoped first release.

Should I spend my whole budget on the first version?

No. Spend a share on the first release and keep the rest for what you learn after launch: fixes, the features users ask for and running costs. A product that launches with no money left for iteration usually stalls.

What costs come after the app is built?

Hosting, third-party services such as maps, messaging and payments, app store accounts, monitoring, security updates and ongoing improvements. Plan these as a monthly or yearly line from the start, not as a surprise after launch.

Should I tell a development company my budget?

Yes. Sharing a budget range lets a good team propose what fits inside it and tell you honestly if it does not. Hiding it usually leads to quotes for very different projects that are hard to compare.
